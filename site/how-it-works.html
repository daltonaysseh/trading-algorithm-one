How this works

Every analyzed call is read in full and scored 0–100 on five independently-judged communication dimensions. The composite score is just their mean — it isn't a separate, independently-judged number. This page explains what each dimension is actually looking for, why it's relevant to equity research, and what it looked like in three real calls.

What this IS

A read of how confident and transparent management sounded on one specific call — the actual dimension scores, tied to real quotes from the transcript.

What this ISN'T

A stock price predictor, a buy/sell signal, a substitute for reading or listening to the actual call, or investment advice of any kind.

The five dimensions

Guidance

Are they confident enough to raise targets, or just reaffirming?

Guidance is one of the few forward-looking signals management controls directly — unlike the quarter just reported, they choose what to say about the one ahead. A specific raise is a real commitment they'll be held to next quarter. Reaffirming after a beat isn't automatically weak, but it's worth noticing when it happens without a stated reason.

"We are raising our fiscal Year 2026 sales outlook. Barring unforeseen economic, global trade, or tariffs-related disruptions, we now expect revenue to grow by over $110 million. $10 million more than we communicated last quarter." David Dunbar, Chairman & CEO — SXI Q1 2026 · scored 85/100 on this dimension
A specific, numbered raise to forward guidance -- the clearest strong-guidance example in the calibration batch.

Hedging

Are they committing to statements, or leaving themselves an out?

Qualifiers aren't inherently dishonest — real uncertainty exists and should sound uncertain. The signal is density and placement: heavy hedging concentrated on the questions an analyst actually cares about is different from routine caution sprinkled through boilerplate risk language.

"That's an interesting question. Obviously, there'd be a lot of different factors playing into the market... I think it's hard to specifically answer that question down to a fine detail... We don't have an exact impact of what that would look like at this point." Joe Marino, CFO — PBF Q3 2025 · scored 30/100 on this dimension
Asked directly to normalize net debt for one-time items, twice -- answered both times with qualifiers instead of a number. The clearest low-hedging-score example (i.e. heavily hedged language) in the batch.

Q&A directness

When asked a specific question, do they give a specific answer?

Q&A is the one part of the call that isn't scripted in advance. Evasive Q&A often signals genuine uncertainty about forward guidance, not just a communications preference — management usually can answer if the number is one they're comfortable with.

"I would characterize that as about 3 basis points per 25 basis point rate cut... when you think about the net interest margin this quarter, we were at 3.91. We had the one interest recovery. If you kind of normalize for that, we're in the high 3.80s. With a couple of few rate cuts that go into the fourth quarter, we would tick down to maybe about 3.80." David Burg, CFO — WSFS Q3 2025 · scored 90/100 on this dimension
A margin-sensitivity question answered with exact basis-point figures and a worked-through calculation, not a general statement.

Consistency

Does the confident script survive contact with unscripted questions?

Prepared remarks are written and rehearsed; Q&A isn't. A gap between the two — upbeat framing in the script, evasion on the same topic under questioning — is harder to fake than either half alone, which makes it one of the more useful single signals in the rubric.

Prepared remarks: "...all of which should come together to create a dynamic environment for the company and our shareholders." Q&A (same call, same underlying question asked twice): "It's hard to put a fine point on that... we don't have an exact impact of what that would look like at this point." Matt Lucey, CEO (prepared remarks) vs. Joe Marino, CFO (Q&A) — PBF Q3 2025 · scored 20/100 on this dimension
The clearest prepared-remarks-vs-Q&A divergence in the batch -- confident, upbeat framing in the scripted portion, evasive on the one specific number analysts pressed for in Q&A. Exactly the pattern the consistency dimension is designed to catch.

Headwinds

Do they name the problem and put a number on it, or wave at it?

Quantifying a headwind ('$10M of higher costs') is a testable claim you can check against next quarter's results. Vague acknowledgment ('challenging environment') costs nothing to say and commits management to nothing — it's the easiest thing to get right on a good call and the easiest thing to fake on a bad one.

"Versus the third quarter last year, we've done well to offset $10 million of higher costs, including tariff costs, raw material increases, energy, and temporary increases like higher logistics costs associated with our U.S. cat litter plant upgrade." Erik Aldag, CFO — MTX Q3 2025 · scored 85/100 on this dimension
Risks named specifically and quantified in dollar terms, rather than described generically -- the rubric's 80-anchor for headwinds.
